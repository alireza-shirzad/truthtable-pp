\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelims}

We introduce notation and cryptographic background required to understand our protocols, most of which we adapt from $\truthtable$~\cite{cryptoeprint:2026/1260}.

\parhead{Boolean hypercube} The $\mu$-dimensional Boolean hypercube $\BoolHCube{\mu}$ is the set $\{0,1\}^\mu$; it has $2^\mu$ points. For a positive integer $m$ we write $[m] := \{0, \dots, m-1\}$. We commonly identify the points of $\BoolHCube{\mu}$ with the integers in $[2^\mu]$, reading $X_1$ as the most significant bit, so that the $i$-th point of $\BoolHCube{\mu}$ is the integer $i-1$.

\parhead{Tables} A \emph{table} is a collection of columns of equal length together with an \emph{activator}, a boolean column marking its active rows (\cref{sec:general-approach}). For a table $\Table$ we write $\Table[\mathsf{x}, \mathsf{y}]$ for its projection onto the columns $\mathsf{x}, \mathsf{y}$, which keeps the activator of $\Table$.

\parhead{Polynomials}
We write $p \in \F^{\le d}[X_1,\dots,X_\mu]$ (or, concisely, $p \in \F^{\le d}[\mathbf{X}]$) to denote a polynomial in $\mu$ variables over the field $\F$, where each variable has degree at most $d$. 


\parhead{Multilinear extensions}
Let $v \in \F^{2^\mu}$ be a vector of field elements, for some $\mu \in \mathbb{N}$.
The \emph{multilinear extension} $\tilde{v}$ of $v$ is the unique multilinear polynomial whose evaluations at the $i$-th point in the $\mu$-dimensional boolean hypercube equal the $i$-th element of $v$.
We now recall some useful multivariate polynomials.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item The \emph{index polynomial} 
        $ \idx_{\mu}(X) \;:=\; \sum_{i=1}^{\mu} 2^{\mu-i} X_i
        $
        maps each point of $\BoolHCube{\mu}$ to the integer it represents.
    \item The \emph{s-contiguous} polynomial
        $
        \contigmus(X) \;:=\; \sum_{i=1}^{\mu} s_i(1 - X_i) \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} \left(s_jX_j + (1 - s_j)(1 - X_j)\right)   
        $ is $1$ for the first $s$ points in $\BoolHCube{\mu}$ and $0$ elsewhere. Here, $s_i$ is the $i$-th most significant bit of $s$. We provide a derivation of this polynomial in \cref{sec:contigmus-derivation}.
\end{itemize}

\parhead{Polynomial oracles}
Algorithms with \emph{oracle} access to a polynomial $p$ (denoted $\Oracle{p})$ can query $p$ at any point $x \in \F^{\mu}$ to obtain the evaluation $p(x)$.

\parhead{Polynomial commitments} A polynomial commitment scheme is a cryptographic primitive that allows one to generate a succinct commitment $\Commitment_p$ to a polynomial $p$, and later reveal its evaluation at specific points, along with a proof that the evaluation is correct~\cite{KateZG10}. 

\parhead{SNARKs via polynomials}
We follow $\truthtable$, which uses the now-standard methodology for constructing SNARKs by combining \emph{Polynomial Interactive Oracle Proofs} (PIOPs) with \emph{Polynomial Commitment} (PC) schemes~\cite{BunzFS20,ChiesaHMMVW20}. A PIOP is an idealized interactive proof between a prover $\PIOPProver$ and a verifier $\PIOPVerifier$, in which the prover sends polynomial oracles and the verifier queries these oracles before deciding acceptance. 
$\truthtable$ builds PIOPs that prove correct execution of relational operators over database tables, and builds these protocols compositionally using polynomial interactive oracle \emph{reductions}~\cite{BenSassonCGGRS19,BunzMNW25b,BawejaMMS25}, which interactively reduce complex claims to simpler algebraic subclaims. As shown in \cref{sec:deferred-piops}, each operator-level protocol ultimately reduces to a small collection of low-level algebraic claims, which are proven using standard PIOPs from the literature. For completeness, we list the protocols used throughout the paper together with the relation each proves; their formal definitions are deferred to \cref{sec:low-level-piops-deferred,sec:deferred-piops}. The first three are the algebraic core, and the remaining ones are column-level checks that reduce to them; columns and their active entries are defined in \cref{sec:general-approach}.

\parhead{Protocol Toolbox}\textbf{\hyperref[{piop:sumcheck}]{Sumcheck}} proves claims of the form $\sum_{x \in \BoolHCube{\mu}} p(x) = s$ for a polynomial $p$ and field element $s$; \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:zerocheck}]{Zerocheck}} proves that a polynomial $p$ vanishes on the Boolean hypercube; and \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:ratsumcheck}]{Rational Sumcheck}} proves claims of the form $\sum_{x \in \BoolHCube{\mu}} p(x)/q(x) = s$ under the promise that $q$ does not vanish on it. The column checks \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:nozero-check}]{NoZero}}, \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:boolean-check}]{Booleanity}}, \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:range-check}]{Range}} and \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:sign-check}]{Sign Check}} prove that every active entry of a column is, respectively, nonzero, in $\{0,1\}$, in $[0, 2^\alpha-1]$ for a given width $\alpha$, or non-negative, positive, non-positive or negative within a given width. \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:zero-indicator}]{Zero Indicator Check}} and \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:cmp-indicator}]{Comparison Indicator Check}} prove that a boolean column marks exactly the zero entries, respectively the non-negative entries within a given width, of a column; the latter turns a comparison into an indicator that other checks can multiply into activators. \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:limb-check}]{Limb Reconstruction Check}} proves that each entry of a column is the concatenation of the corresponding entries of $k$ narrower limb columns. \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:lookup-check}]{Lookup Check}} proves $\col_1 \lookup \col_2$, i.e., that every active value of $\col_1$ occurs among the active values of $\col_2$; applied to random linear combinations of several columns, it proves that every active row of one table occurs in another. \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:mset}]{Multiset Equality Check}} proves that two columns (or tables) hold the same multiset of active values (or rows), and \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:keyedsumcheck}]{Keyed Sumcheck}} proves that two tables with key and value columns have, for every key, the same sum of active values; it underlies grouped aggregation and the multiset check. \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:rotcheck}]{Rotation Check}} proves that one column is the cyclic rotation of another by a given shift, which aligns every row with its neighbour. Finally, \textbf{\hyperref[{piop:lex-sort}]{Lexicographic Sort Check}} proves that the rows of a multi-column integer table are in non-decreasing (or strictly increasing) lexicographic order over a given set of adjacent row pairs, returning the pairs that remain tied; this is the sorting protocol of $\truthtable$~\cite{cryptoeprint:2026/1260}.

To obtain a SNARK, $\truthtable$ compiles these PIOPs using a polynomial commitment scheme~\cite{ChiesaHMMVW20,BunzFS20}. Intuitively, this compilation emulates the polynomial oracles in the PIOP model: each polynomial oracle sent by the prover is replaced by a \emph{polynomial commitment}, and each oracle query is replaced by a proof that the committed polynomial evaluates to the claimed value at the verifier’s chosen point~\cite{ChiesaHMMVW20,GabizonWC19}. Since this compilation applies generically, it can be treated as a black box: the resulting SNARK inherits its efficiency and soundness directly from the underlying PIOPs and commitment scheme.
